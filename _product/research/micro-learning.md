# Micro-learning: how awareness products teach, and what works for people 70+

How security-awareness vendors build short lessons, what senior-facing scam education does, what research says about older learners and about whether training works, and what Prudence's Learn tab takes from it. Researched 2026-10-29 in one short pass: vendor pages and studies were checked at the source unless marked "(unverified)". SoSafe, Arsen, Mantra, Wizer, Curricula, Ninjio, CybSafe, Living Security, usecure and Cyberlift were not checked this round.

## Riot (tryriot.com, the "Albert" coach)
- Format: a chat with Albert, an "AI cyber coach", in Slack, Teams, Google Chat or a web page. Riot's own rules for a course: "always start with a great story" (a real incident, such as the intern whose server password was "solarwinds123"), write the course as a dialogue, keep it relevant, then test with phishing drills. Albert is written to be "interested, interesting, and maybe even annoying from time to time".
- Length: "3-minute micro-lessons" (landing page), "3-5 minutes" (founder, 2021), "punchy five-minute conversations" (blog), 5-10 minutes (lastminute.com, a customer).
- Questions: answers picked from buttons or menus (TechCrunch 2023); a deepfake course asks the learner to tell a real voice from a generated one. Lessons open on the learner's own data ("your email address can be found in five different data breaches") and use the real CEO's name in CEO-fraud scenarios.
- Gamification: none inside the lessons; a "Karma score" for each employee's security posture, and gamification announced for the posture platform (TechCrunch 2025). The founder's goal: "as entertaining as learning a new language on Duolingo".
- Cadence: "all year long"; at lastminute.com, 24 sessions a year plus a simulated phishing email each quarter. After a failed simulation Albert shows the employee the exact email and the clues they missed. "Nudges" turn a news article into a refresher.
- For managers: progress metrics, an alert when someone "needs extra support", compliance exports (Vanta, Drata).
- Claims (vendor or customer): 85% and "91%+" completion; lastminute.com's vulnerability index fell below half of its starting value in 12 months (1,700 staff); 1 million employees at 1,500 companies (2025).
- Price: $6.89 per employee per month, billed yearly (1-200 employees); a free start.

## Elba (elba.security, the "Sam" assistant)
- Our three screenshots of an Elba lesson in Slack show the pattern: short messages one at a time with bold keywords ("Today, data breaches are one of the most expensive forms of cybersecurity incidents - let's break down what's really at stake"); answer buttons, some with an emoji, that move the story ("What's a data breach exactly?", "For financial gain", "What should I do then?", "I'll check!"); a reaction that confirms ("Exactly! Put simply - data is money!"); a screenshot as evidence (a leaked LinkedIn password dump, the Have I Been Pwned site); "3 tips to follow" as pointing-hand bullets; one real action (check your email on Have I Been Pwned); a sign-off ("Stay sharp - your company's security depends on you", "Click on the button above to resume").
- Public pages confirm the frame: Sam delivers "short, recurring training and phishing simulations" in Slack, Teams or Google Chat, with email as the fallback. The lesson builder mixes text, images and multiple-choice questions and drafts a lesson with AI from a prompt or a PDF; a learner who passes the quiz can skip the course; 8 languages including French; reminders twice a week to monthly; managers see "Overdue training" for their reports.
- Gamification: none described beyond quiz scores and a "Security leaderboard" that the docs name without describing. Tone: second person, emoji, light (our screenshots). Price: on demo only.

## Phished (phished.io)
- Phished Academy: "bite-sized", "interactive microlearnings based on gamification", sessions of "only a few minutes", levels of rising difficulty, checkpoint tests that reward participation, certificates graded Bronze to Platinum (Advanced plan).
- Simulations: "continuous, AI-driven", difficulty adapting per user; repeat-offender training; Threat Alerts when a global threat appears.
- Behavioural Risk Score: a per-person, team and company score "predicting the likelihood of a cyber incident due to human error", fed by simulations, training, the learner's apps and devices and threat alerts (All-in-One plan). The weighting is not published.
- Price: quote only (Core, Advanced, All-in-One).

## The field
- Hoxhunt: a simulation "roughly every 10 days" (email, SMS, Teams, phone, deepfake video) and a micro-lesson "at the moment they report or click"; stars, badges, streaks and leaderboards; "positive, non-punitive coaching"; difficulty adapts per user; 40+ languages. Vendor figures: 60% of users report a real malicious email within 12 months; 66% opt in to extra lessons. Reviewers say leaderboard points push people to race simulations and that a late report costs points (review snippets, unverified). Price on quote.
- KnowBe4: the largest library (videos, games, "The Inside Man", a drama series in its seventh season in 2026); optional leaderboards (by team and, since Feb 2024, by person for reported phish) and badges in its Learner Experience. About $20-50 per seat per year at list (Vendr estimate, unverified).
- Not checked this round, from memory (unverified): SoSafe (story-based gamified e-learning and simulations); Arsen and Mantra (French; simulations including SMS and voice, short tips); Wizer (1-2 minute videos with one question, humor, a free tier); Curricula, now Huntress (cartoon episodes with recurring characters); Ninjio (3-4 minute anime episodes from real breaches, monthly); CybSafe (behavioural-science nudges); Living Security (team escape rooms); usecure (short video courses, public per-user pricing); Cyberlift (not researched).

## Senior-facing scam education
- AARP Fraud Watch quizzes: 8 single-answer multiple-choice questions; after each, "CORRECT" or "INCORRECT" and an "Answer:" paragraph; a running count, a hint toggle, a score at the end; friendly, sources named (FTC, BBB).
- Jigsaw (Google) phishing quiz (2019): 8 realistic emails, each judged "phishing or legitimate", with an explanation after each answer.
- Scam Spotter (Google and the Cybercrime Support Network, 2020): three golden rules, "Slow it down", "Spot check", "Stop! Don't send", and a quiz meant to be taken with a senior ("compare your scores").
- Take Five (UK Finance): "Stop, Challenge and Protect". Its "Are you scam-savvy?" quiz asks 9 yes/no decisions on a realistic text, email, advert or call script, then shows "Correct!" or "Incorrect!" and the same explanation either way, with no score. Its 2018 "Too Smart To Be Scammed?" test: 9% of 63,000 people got every answer right (press snippet, unverified).
- FTC Pass It On (2014, refreshed 2023 and 2026): older adults are "part of the solution to the problem, not just the victims of scammers"; fact sheets, bookmarks, word games, presentations on 13 topics, videos of an older adult's own story; print matters.
- Cyber-Seniors: young volunteers teach seniors on Zoom, including a grandparent-scam session, one trainer and four mentors per 20 seniors (unverified). Senior Planet (OATS from AARP): one-hour lectures that walk through sample emails, texts and deepfakes (unverified). Age UK: the "Avoiding scams" guide, audio versions, local talks (unverified). Friends Against Scams (UK): an 8-minute video, an online session, group talks (unverified).
- France: cybermalveillance.gouv.fr's SensCyber, open to the public since March 2024: 3 units (Comprendre, Agir, Transmettre) of about 25-45 minutes each, quizzes with a 60% pass mark, a certificate (press, unverified). No senior track; nothing senior-specific found on Pix.
- Pattern: realistic example messages, one short rule set, an explanation after every answer, print and audio, and people (volunteers, family). None uses points, streaks or leaderboards; none builds a lesson from the learner's own messages.

## What research says about older learners
- People 75+ and game elements: in interviews and storyboards (N=18, mean age 84.6), the main motivation to play was socializing; participants avoided competition, preferred collaboration and care-taking, and found badges and points "meaningless", with a visibility that "puts participants under pressure" (Altmeyer, Lessel, Kruger, DIS 2018).
- Review of gamification for people 55+: 12 studies, 11 in health, 10 with positive results, "mostly weak indications of positive effects" (Koivisto and Malik, The Gerontologist 2020).
- Warnings work, then fade: AARP volunteers phoned past fraud victims (52% of those followed up were 70+) with a warning; a mock scam call two or four weeks later was accepted less often than by controls (almost 50%). A warning about the same scam lost its effect by four weeks; a warning about a different scam did not (Scheibe et al. 2014).
- Short lessons work, then need a reminder: a 3-minute video, or the same content as text, cut susceptibility to investment fraud at once (N=2,000 US adults); six months later the effect was gone unless people got a reminder at three months, which kept it (Burke, Kieffer, Mottola, Perez-Arce, FINRA Foundation 2021).
- Answering questions helps memory at any age: the testing effect holds for older adults (Meyer and Logan 2013, unverified). Repeating a false claim in order to debunk it can make older adults remember it as true days later (Skurnik et al. 2005, unverified): show the clue and the rule, never the scammer's sentence as a statement to judge.
- Processing speed slows with age, so timers and countdowns penalise older learners (general ageing literature, unverified); narration plus text helps older learners more than text alone (Van Gerven et al. 2006, unverified).
- Duolingo-style mechanics (from the app, unverified): streaks with freezes, hearts lost on each mistake (being replaced by "energy" in 2025), XP, weekly leagues. For 70+: keep the short habit and the instant feedback; leave hearts (punish mistakes), leagues (competition), XP (points seen as meaningless) and guilt reminders.

## Does training work?
Vendor numbers are large: KnowBe4's simulated-phishing failure rate falls from 33.1% before training to 4.1% after 12 months (67.7 million simulations, its own data); Hoxhunt and Riot customers report similar falls. Independent field studies are much weaker. At UC San Diego Health (19,500 employees, 8 months, 10 campaigns), annual training showed no significant relationship with clicking, training shown after a click cut clicks by about 2%, 75% of users spent a minute or less on the training page and a third closed it at once, and by month eight more than half had clicked at least once (Ho et al., IEEE S&P 2025). At a company of 56,000 (14,733 participants, 15 months), training shown after a failed simulation made employees more susceptible, while asking everyone to report suspicious emails worked (Lain, Kostiainen, Capkun, IEEE S&P 2022). For older adults, a warning or a 3-minute lesson works for weeks, not months, unless it is repeated. Lessons cannot be the protection: the verdict at the moment of the message is. They earn their place when tied to real messages, repeated, and measured with the comprehension test; never claim that lessons reduce scams.

## What we take for Prudence
1. Lesson format: "story then question", 5 steps, about one minute, one screen per step, one rule per lesson, one question per step.
   1. Story: one realistic message, the senior's own flagged one when we have it, otherwise a sample with their real providers ("A text says your Colissimo parcel is stuck: pay 1,99 EUR").
   2. Question: "What do you do?", 2 or 3 large answers.
   3. Feedback: warm whatever the answer, then the clue marked on the message (the link, the hurry, the payment).
   4. Rule: one sentence plus the "the real company never" line, with a Listen button.
   5. Check and finish: a second message, "Scam or real?" (one tap), then "Rule 3 of 8 learned" and an optional "Tell Sophie".
   Elba's chat rhythm (one short message at a time, buttons that move the story, one real action at the end) fits; its long scroll does not: one step per screen.
2. Question types, three at most: "What do you do?" (2-3 choices); "Scam or real?" (2 choices on a realistic message); later "Tap the clue" (tap the part of the message that gives it away). No free text, no drag and drop, no timers.
3. Wrong answers: never "Incorrect!" in red. "Many careful people would tap that. Here is the clue." Show the right answer and go on: no lives, no retry wall. Never present the scammer's claim as a statement to judge true or false.
4. Gamification that fits 70+: progress ("Rule 3 of 8"); a wall of the rules I know (cards to reread); a short warm finish (a check mark, an optional brief confetti); each rule coming back after 3 days, a week and a month (spaced review, after Burke et al.); sharing with the trusted person ("Mom learned the parcel rule"), because care-taking and family motivate more than scores. Leave out: points or XP, badges, leaderboards, levels that lock lessons, hearts or lives, timers, streaks that can be lost, guilt notifications, certificates, any comparison between seniors.
5. Tone and humor: laugh at the scammer, never at the senior; no age jokes; "you" and "your"; plain words ("fake message", not "phishing"; "code", not "2FA"); one idea per sentence; end calm, never on fear. Two example lines:
   - "Your bank already has your money. It does not need your code as well."
   - "Microsoft does not watch your screen and phone you when it sneezes."
6. Read-aloud: every step has a large Listen button that reads the step in the lesson's language; each step short enough to read aloud in about 10 seconds; nothing plays before a tap.
7. EN and FR: each YAML lesson carries an `en` and an `fr` version written for each country, not translated word for word: local brands (USPS, UPS, Amazon; La Poste, Colissimo, Chronopost, Ameli, impots.gouv), local payment tells (gift cards; coupons PCS, Transcash, Neosurf), local actions (call the number on your card; forward spam texts to 7726 in the US, 33700 in France), "vous" in French.
8. Demo: after the red card, "Learn why in 1 minute": the story, one tap, warm feedback, the rule read aloud. Fifteen seconds.

### Eight lesson topics, ranked for 70+ in the US and France
Ranked by how often a 70+ person meets the scam in email or texts, the money lost (research/scam-market-stats.md), and whether one rule fits one minute.

| # | Topic | The one rule | Funniest honest angle | The real company never |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Urgency | When a message rushes you, slow down: anything real can wait ten minutes. | Your bank has been open for 150 years; it can wait until you finish your coffee. | Your bank, Amazon or the tax office never gives you 30 minutes to act or lose everything. |
| 2 | Package fees | A parcel fee by text is a scam: check in the shop's own app or site. | The parcel you never ordered wants to reach you very badly, for just $1.99. | USPS, UPS, La Poste and Chronopost never text you a link to pay a fee. |
| 3 | "The bank calls you" | Hang up and call the number on the back of your card. | The "fraud department" calling to protect you from fraud is the fraud. | Your bank never asks you to move money to a "safe account" or to read out a code. |
| 4 | Codes and passwords | A code sent to you is for your eyes only: never read it out or type it for someone. | A code is like a toothbrush: you do not share it, not even with a polite man from the bank. | No real company asks you to read back a code it just sent you, or for your password. |
| 5 | Tech-support pop-ups | A screen that shouts "virus, call this number" is the scam: close it and call no one. | Microsoft does not watch your screen and phone you when it sneezes. | Microsoft, Apple and Norton never put a phone number in a warning or ask to take control of your computer. |
| 6 | Grandchild in trouble | Hang up and call your grandchild on the number you already have. | Your grandson has a new number, a broken nose and a sudden need for gift cards. | A real lawyer or police officer never asks the family for cash, a courier or gift cards. |
| 7 | Gift cards | Anyone who wants to be paid in gift cards is a scammer. | The tax office does not take supermarket gift cards, however carefully you scratch them. | No government office, utility or company is ever paid in gift cards or PCS or Transcash coupons. |
| 8 | Romance and the "crypto friend" | Never send money or crypto to someone you have only met online. | He is a surgeon on an oil rig, and somehow always one customs fee away from meeting you. | A real friend never asks for money, for crypto, or for secrecy from your family. |

Left out as separate lessons: "we noticed a breach" (folded into lesson 4: a real security alert never asks for your password) and fake invoices (folded into lesson 5: a surprise $399 renewal that gives a phone number "to cancel").

## Lessons as stories (2026-11-04)
The first Prudence lessons explained the trick and then asked three questions: a lecture, then a test. What keeps people inside a one-minute lesson is a story with taps: Duolingo's Stories (a short dialogue, one line at a time, a tap question every few lines, recurring characters with running jokes) and the inoculation games from Cambridge ("Bad News", "Harmony Square"), where the player hears the manipulator think and learns the trick from the inside (Roozenbeek and van der Linden, 2019). What Prudence takes: the scammer's text lands on the screen under the name he borrows, the person decides, the scammer reacts in his own voice, the narrator stays literal (irony is a risk for readers who take words at face value), and a wrong choice plays out for one screen before a rewind to the same choice. Sources: duolingo.com/stories; Roozenbeek, J. and van der Linden, S. (2019), "Fake news game confers psychological resistance against online misinformation", Palgrave Communications 5, 65, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-019-0279-9.
